Every tank water heater has a limited life, and most reach the end somewhere between year eight and year twelve. How long yours lasts in Iowa City comes down to water hardness, water pressure, and whether the tank was ever flushed or its anode rod replaced. The signs tend to repeat: rust-colored hot water, popping and rumbling from sediment, a shower that turns cold sooner every month, and, most urgently, water around the base of the tank. That one ends the debate. A rusted-through inner tank cannot be patched or sealed, so a new heater is the only real fix.

A new water heater should fit the way your household uses hot water today, not the way it did when the old one went in. That is why Iowa City Water Heater asks a few questions first: how many people shower in the morning, whether you fill a large tub, and how many bathrooms draw at once. For tank heaters we compare first-hour ratings; for tankless, we size to the gallons per minute you need at the temperature rise your incoming water demands in the coldest months. For a heat pump model, we check space and airflow, because it draws its heat from the surrounding room. Gas line, vent, and panel capacity are all confirmed ahead of time, so installation day holds no surprises.

How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you money and keep a slow drip from turning into a flooded utility room. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • Repairs that keep coming back, one part after another
  • Water leaking from the bottom or body of the tank
  • Rust flaking from the tank jacket or its connections
  • A heater older than most tanks last, usually eight to twelve years
  • A household that has outgrown the tank's capacity
  • Loud rumbling or popping that flushing no longer fixes
  • Discolored hot water, a sign the tank is corroding inside
  • Energy bills rising while hot water gets harder to come by

Why Hire a Professional for Water Heater Replacement?

A water heater replacement touches more systems than most people expect. On the gas side there is the supply line, the sediment trap, the shutoff, and a vent that has to draft properly so exhaust never backs up into the house. Electrical work means matching breaker and wire gauge to the element wattage. A correctly rated relief valve has to be piped to a safe discharge point, and depending on your home, code may require a thermal expansion tank, a drain pan, earthquake straps, or a permit and inspection. Tankless and heat pump models add their own demands: bigger gas lines, sealed vent systems, heavy electrical loads, and condensate drainage.

Professional replacement gets all of it right the first time. Our experienced technicians size the heater to your household, verify the gas line, vent, and electrical service, and install whatever code items apply. We leak-test, check draft and carbon monoxide on gas units, and set a safe temperature before we leave. It is what separates a heater that runs quietly for years from one that has problems from the start. And when a repair would give your current tank several more good years, we say so instead of selling you a new one.

Simple Ways to Extend Your Water Heater's Life

A few simple habits, like an annual flush and a quick look at the relief valve, can add years to a tank. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Flush the new tank once or twice a year to keep sediment from building up
  • Keep the temperature near 120 degrees for safety and slower mineral buildup
  • Write the install date on the tank so you know when to plan the next replacement
  • Clean the air filter on a heat pump water heater every few months
  • Descale a tankless unit every year, or more often with hard water
  • Have water pressure checked; above about 80 psi, a pressure reducing valve protects the new heater
  • A fresh anode rod at the right time is the cheapest way to extend tank life
